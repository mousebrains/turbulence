The glider speed problem

On six of seven deployments, taking the EM flowmeter at face value requires a negative angle of attack — which a wing cannot have. Seven years of data, five flowmeters, and four failed explanations later, here is what is established, what is excluded, and the one bench measurement that would end it.

01

The geometry, settled

Let θ be the elevation of the vehicle's long axis from horizontal, γ the same for the velocity vector, and α the angle of attack — all magnitudes. The relation is

So U = |W| / sin(θ + α) with α positive, which is what ODAS and perturb implement. Confirmed independently on RU33, the only vehicle that records both legs: at the same |θ| its dive and climb give α = +6.48° and +6.04°, agreeing to 0.36 ± 0.26°. Under the wrong convention they would have differed by 2α ≈ 13°, which is 50σ away.

The one-line consequence

Since α ≥ 0, the ratio excess = U_EM·sin θ / |W| can never exceed 1. It is a model-free upper bound. Any deployment with excess > 1 has a discrepancy that no admissible angle of attack can absorb.

03

Four dead ends

Each of these looked convincing at the time. All four are recorded here so nobody re-runs them.

HypothesisHow it died
Local flow acceleration around the vehicle body Refuted by geometry. The EM head is at the front of the MR, the MR sits on top of the glider, and the sensor tips are ahead of the glider's nose. A body accelerates flow alongside itself, not ahead of its stagnation point — ahead of a nose the flow is if anything decelerated, biasing U_EM low. Wrong sign.
A nominal 2023 EM slope SN 044 and 046 were calibrated the same day and differ by 2.27%, so JAC does issue per-unit slopes. SN 042 is a 12.8%-low outlier, so anchoring a rescale to it was the error. Rescaling to the cluster mean — where the units already sit — moves α by 0.02°.
Roll (the gliders fly 0–13° of it) MR435 + EM 066 gives excess 1.0430 (2025) and 1.0434 (2026) — 0.04% apart across a year and a 10° roll change. Within osu685 the roll trend even runs opposite to the between-deployment one, the signature of a confound with depth.
The on-deck reading is the zero It rank-ordered perfectly with the needed correction (ρ = +1.00, n = 4) and EM 066's year-on-year drift matched to 0.8 mm/s — but a proper regression with an adequate speed lever puts the intercept at zero, not at the deck value. Retracted; see §5.
Why the deck reading cannot be the whole zero anyway

A Faraday flowmeter measures an EMF induced in a conducting fluid. In air the electrodes see an open circuit, so you read the amplifier's input offset into a near-infinite source impedance. In water you additionally get the electrochemical half-cell potential between the two electrodes — the classic EM zero-drift term, the one that grows with fouling, and the one air cannot show.

05

Why the field data cannot settle it

Write the model with a sensor offset c. In steady glide at fixed θ, 

sin(θ + α) = |W| / (U_EM − c)        rearranged:      U_EM = [1/sin(θ+α)]·|W| + c

— a straight line through the origin if the sensor is unbiased. So a regression of U_EM on |W| returns both unknowns: the slope carries α, the intercept is the offset. No angle of attack need be assumed. The lever is the range of speeds the glider flies at fixed pitch, which exists because buoyancy changes with depth.

That is the right test. It does not converge:

The large positive intercepts come only from the short levers, which extrapolate 2.5–4× beyond their data. With an adequate lever the intercept is consistent with zero. And the slope and intercept trade off, so α swings 3.8° on MR433 2026 depending on whether an intercept is allowed — while that intercept is only 1.6σ from zero.

A useful diagnostic, though

The through-origin estimate is rock-stable — it moves by 0.3° on MR433 and 0.02° on MR435 as the |W| floor is swept from 0.05 to 0.15 m/s — while the free-intercept fit swings wildly. The data cannot support two parameters. Use the constrained estimator and report the offset as unknown, rather than fitting one and believing it.

RU33: both legs, and why they still cannot settle it

Two legs at different pitch give two equations in (α, c) with no AOA assumed. RU33 is the only vehicle that records both, and it returns c = −0.010 m/s [−0.030, +0.011] — consistent with zero — and α = +5.0° [+3.0, +7.4], bracketing both reference values.

Do not rely on that number

RU33 flies its dives and climbs at approximately the same pitch and the same speed, which is exactly the condition under which the two-leg solve has no lever. The entire signal is a 0.50° difference in |θ| between climb (25.475°) and dive (25.975°) — and an inclinometer zero bias shifts |θ| oppositely on the two legs, mimicking precisely that. At ±0.5° estimated mounting tolerance, the lever and the error are the same size.

So the RU33 result is consistent with a zero offset and a physical α, but it does not measure either. Both legs is not the lever people assume it is — a spread in |θ| is, and flying both legs at the same attitude provides none.

What the two legs do establish is that the flight itself is symmetric: W/U is 0.522 on the climb and 0.534 on the dive, so the glide angle is the same within 0.8°, and the per-leg α agree to 0.36 ± 0.26°. The legs differ in absolute speed (U_EM 0.326 vs 0.275 m/s) — normal for a glider trimmed with different buoyancy each way — but not in geometry. That symmetry is what validates the §1 sign convention; it is not a second constraint on the offset.

06

Filtering the training set

Non-negotiable, in the order they matter. Together they discard ~63% of the in-band 2026 data.

  1. Thruster off, and known off (m_thruster_current == 0). Powered flight is not a glide and breaks the force balance the whole method rests on. Two traps: the sensor reports only every ~56 s, so give it its own large max_gap (7200 s) or the zero-order hold gets blanked and the state is lost; and an unknown period must be discarded, not assumed off.
  2. Sign consistency — nose-up and rising, or nose-down and sinking. A vehicle pitched up while sinking is stalled. Inside a θ 43–46° band this is already satisfied: the stalls live at low |pitch|, near the inflections.
  3. A |W| floor, swept not chosen. The low-|W| tail is both where the intercept lever lives and where marginal flight lives. Sweeping it exposes the trade-off; picking one value hides it.
  4. Steadiness — rolling sd of θ < 2° and of W < 0.02 over 60 s. The derivation assumes equilibrium, so acceleration must be excluded rather than averaged over.
Also correct, and one-signed

dP/dt is dbar/s, but the flight model needs m/s. One dbar is 10⁴/(ρg) metres — 0.9955 m at the surface, ~0.991 m at 500 m. So W in m/s is 0.5–1.0% smaller, which raises the excess by the same fraction and makes the discrepancy slightly worse.

07

The bench test that ends this

Every in-situ lever available is between 1.3× and 5×. Two tanks give a lever of ~100×. That is why the bench can settle what seven deployments could not.

The model

c(σ) = A + B/σ

  A   amplifier input offset, plus electrode asymmetry that does not scale
  B/σ bias current × source impedance;  Z_source ∝ 1/σ

Two measurements, two unknowns — exactly determined. Seawater is ~4–5 S/m, freshwater ~0.05 S/m. Freshwater is not merely a second point: it is a ~100× amplifier for the bias-current term. If B matters at all, fresh water will show it unmistakably. If fresh ≈ salt, then A dominates, there is no σ-scaling to do, and a single seawater zero is the entire answer — which is itself worth knowing.

The rig

A horizontal, fully insulated box with the MicroRider lying in cradles, and only its cable through the lid. Horizontal is the important part: it puts the sensing axis perpendicular to the buoyant plume that the MR's own 0.5–1 W of dissipation drives along its body.

Clearance is the only physical constraint, and it is generous. MR is ~1 m long, 11 cm dia.

  • Raise the cradles so the MR is centred vertically. Resting it low makes the floor the closest boundary at a few cm — 5% instead of 1.5%, giving back threefold the margin the box was built for.
  • Non-metallic throughout, and no metal fittings near the head.
  • A sealed thermistor at mid-box, ~50 cm from the head. Unlike a conductivity cell it drives no current through the water — which was the real objection to leaving a CTD logging beside a sensor whose zero you are measuring at the millimetre-per-second level.

Build UP from fresh — do not dilute down from seawater

Start with tap water and add artificial sea salt in doubling steps. One disposal event instead of five, no seawater to transport inland, and the highest-leverage point is measured first, with the cleanest electrodes.

Use an aquarium sea salt (Instant Ocean or similar), not NaCl — those mixes reproduce the major ion ratios (Na, Cl, Mg, SO₄, Ca, K), which is what preserves the electrode-chemistry argument. Each night

  1. Add the salt, stir thoroughly, let it settle. With τ ≈ 15 days, whatever stratification you seal in stays — mixing is the only chance to make it uniform.
  2. Conductivity and temperature before and after, not continuously. σ should rise over the soak by a calculable amount (0.05–0.25% depending on box size and power) as the MR's own heat accumulates — a prediction to check, not a constancy to assume. Log the MR's current draw so the figure can be computed rather than bracketed.
  3. Soak ~16 h. ~100 case time constants; every thermal and electrochemical transient long dead, and the whole record usable.
  4. Reverse the MR end-for-end once during the series. Any residual axial circulation reverses relative to the sensing axis; a true electronic zero does not. Two minutes with cradles, and it turns "the convection is probably down at 1%" into a measurement.
  5. Sweep the wall clearance once — centred, then offset. Same logic. Do it in the tap-water box, where source impedance is ~100× higher and any boundary sensitivity is at its most pronounced.
  6. Bench every unit you can reach, and repeat before and after a deployment. The deployments differ by flowmeter, not by vehicle, so a table of zeros keyed by EM serial is what turns this into a correction — and a before/after pair is the direct test of fouling.
Incl_T is a case thermometer, and here that is enough

The inclinometer sits inside the pressure case. Against the glider CTD it lags the water by ~6.5 min (median 390 s over 21 profiles) and spans only 67% of its range — useless across a 50-minute glider profile. But 16 h is ~120 time constants, so in the box it tracks the water essentially perfectly. Incl_T − T_water is then a direct readout of the MR's self-heating (a constant 12–24 mK). If that difference drifts, the electronics duty-cycled — and the plume pulsed with it.

Two construction facts that settle earlier worries

316 stainless main body. A series conduction path is set by its worst conductor: at k = 16 the fin decay length is 2.7 cm, so 30 cm of immersion is ~11 e-foldings and room-temperature swings reach the head not at all. Anodized aluminium alone would have been 9.7 cm and let ~134 mK through for a 3 °C room excursion.

Electrodes sealed in a stainless housing. No drying-out or re-wetting concern between steps. And because nearly all EM flowmeters use AC excitation with synchronous detection, static half-cell potentials are largely rejected — which leaves the amplifier offset and the impedance-dependent term, i.e. exactly A + B/σ. Worth confirming the excitation really is AC, and at what frequency.

How far from the tank walls?

Two different ranges matter, and only the second one governs a zero measurement.

The flow-sensing volume is small. A probe-type EM sensor weights the surrounding flow with a dipole-like function falling as roughly r⁻³, so the effective sampling volume is on the order of one to two head diameters. For a flow calibration that is what sets the clearance.

The zero is governed by something with longer reach: source impedance. With no flow there is no EMF to distort, so the sensing volume is irrelevant. What a boundary changes is the electrical path between the electrodes — and the bias-current offset is I_b × Z_source, the very term the two-tank test is trying to isolate. So:

  • Use a non-metallic tank. A conducting wall shunts the electrode field and drops Z_source sharply; an insulating wall constrains the return paths and raises it. Metal is much the worse of the two, and steel tanks, rebar and immersed fittings all count.
  • Treat the free surface and the bottom as boundaries too. Air is an insulator; so is most tank lining.
  • Aim for ≥10 head diameters of clearance in every direction as a starting point — the usual EM-current-meter rule of thumb.
Better than a rule of thumb: sweep it

Record the zero at several distances — centred, half way out, and close to a wall. If the reading does not change over the range the tank allows, the clearance is adequate and you have measured that rather than assumed it. If it does change, you have found the scale that matters and can correct or move. It costs about ten minutes and converts an unknown into a bound.

Do the sweep in seawater, where Z_source is lowest and the wall effect should be weakest, and in freshwater, where Z_source is ~100× higher and any boundary sensitivity will be at its most pronounced. If the freshwater zero is wall-dependent, that alone would explain why a fresh-tank number cannot simply be scaled.

The AEM1G's actual head geometry, electrode spacing and field extent are not in any documentation we hold — worth asking JAC or Rockland directly, along with the input impedance and bias current of the front end, which is what sets B in the model above.

What the scaling can and cannot do

The bias-current term should scale cleanly as 1/σ. The electrochemical term will not — different ions, different half-cell potentials, so freshwater electrode chemistry is not seawater chemistry rescaled. Treat the salt-tank reading as the primary operational number and the fresh-tank reading as the diagnostic that says how much of it is bias current.

Targets to test — not values to match

The field fits imply roughly +0.05 m/s (EM 051), +0.02 to +0.03 (EM 066) and near zero (EM 079). Those come from analyses since retracted, so they are hypotheses for the bench to confirm or kill, not numbers to reproduce.

08

Analysing the observations

The two-tank fit

With σ measured at each tank, solve c(σ) = A + B/σ directly. Report A and B with uncertainties, then predict c at in-situ σ for each deployment and re-run the excess table in §2. The success criterion is stated in advance: every deployment should give α > 0. If any stays negative, the offset is not the whole story and this note needs another section.

The σ-dependence in the field is currently untestable

Within the analysis band, conductivity spans only 1.08× (34.2–37.0 mS/cm) and is collinear with depth. The measured correlation runs opposite to the bias-current prediction, but with an 8% lever that is the depth trend in disguise, not a refutation. Only the tanks can separate it.

Still worth doing on the vehicles

  • Record the MicroRider on a subset of dives — but only where the dive pitch DIFFERS from the climb pitch. Both legs at the same attitude buys nothing (see RU33 above); the lever is spread in |θ|, not the leg. On the OSU gliders that spread already exists in normal operation — they dive at ~25° and climb at ~45° — so it is simply not being recorded. Monte Carlo says 10% of yos is enough: α to ±0.13°, against ±0.4° on osu685-as-flown and ±2.3° on osu684. Past 25% the returns vanish.
  • The deeper reason is falsifiability. With one attitude the model cannot fail — two unknowns, one constraint, so some (α, c) always fits and residual structure stays invisible. Two attitudes leave a spare degree of freedom, and the model becomes testable.
  • Ask Rockland what formula the MR firmware uses to turn its 2-axis tilt sensor into Incl_X/Incl_Y. An atan2(a_x, a_z) form is roll-dependent, an asin(a_x) form is not. |Incl_Y| − |m_pitch| grows with roll on two independent instruments, far beyond sensor accuracy (±0.3°) or assembly tolerance (±0.5°) — and it also matters for the RU33 both-legs result above.

Until then

Run both speed arms — em and flight — and report the ratio as the uncertainty rather than picking one silently. On osu684 that ratio is 1.76× in ε; on RU33, where the EM is self-consistent with a physical α, it is 0.91×. Do not apply a single "U_EM is X% high" correction across deployments: the units differ from each other, and the absolute level is unknown.
