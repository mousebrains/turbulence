# current-meters

Calibration and dynamic response of *velocity* sensors — electromagnetic
current meters in particular. Opened for the AEM1-G in-situ calibration work
(`../../../AEM1-G/`), where the question is not how to estimate ε but whether
the along-body speed feeding it is right.

The subject has its own literature, largely from the nearshore community in the
1980s, and it is a different one from the microstructure groups here: the
sensors are EM rather than acoustic or shear, and the recurring failure modes
are gain error, zero offset, angular response, and electronic filtering in the
wave band.

The second table is the flowmeter's own physics (what sets the gain and the
zero, and which of them depends on the water's conductivity). It is the
literature behind the still-water bench test in `scripts/em_bench_zero.py`.
A third table collects the theory of where the gain comes from (the weight
function, the flow pattern near point electrodes, nearby conductors); a fourth,
field performance and intercomparisons, including the acoustic reference.
For how a `U_EM` over-read shows up in glider flight, see
[`../gliders-and-platforms/`](../gliders-and-platforms/README.md).

**What the second table says about the bench model.** `em_bench_zero.py` fits
c(σ) = A + B/σ. The literature supports that split and says what could break
it:

- **Gain** is conductivity-independent (Bevir 1970, Maalouf 2006), provided the
  source resistance stays well below the input resistance (Baker 2016).
- **A zero ∝ 1/σ** has a published mechanism: leakage through the coil
  insulation divided against the electrode source resistance (Griffiths &
  Collar 1980). That is not the mechanism `em_bench_zero.py` names for B/σ
  (amplifier bias current × source impedance), though both scale with source
  resistance and the fit cannot tell them apart. Cushing's patent reports
  conductivity dependence of both meter factor and zero, with no functional
  form.
- **A σ-independent zero** also has one: flux-linkage quadrature leaking through
  the detector (Maalouf 2006b). That belongs in A.
- **The eddy-current and electrode-interface routes** (Maalouf 2007, 2006a)
  depend on the liquid without a stated 1/σ form. The third can tests for them.

## Where the gain comes from: the weight function, the flow pattern and nearby conductors

The flowmeter signal is the velocity integrated over the volume, weighted by
W = B × j (Bevir 1970, above). These papers work out what that weighting does.
Small electrodes weight the flow next to them most, so the gain belongs to the
flow pattern there as much as to the instrument. A conductor or iron within a
few electrode spacings moves the gain by percents to tens of percent. All but
the two probe papers (Zhang & Hemp 1994; Zhang 2020, ch. 4) are about pipe
meters, so carrying them over to a head on a MicroRider nose is by analogy.

[doi:10.1088/0022-3727/19/8/014](https://doi.org/10.1088/0022-3727/19/8/014) | `Hemp_Versteeg_1986_Prediction_Electromagnetic_Flowmeter_Characteristics.pdf` | A Fourier-matrix method for the axisymmetric weight function and for the K-factor (signal per unit mean velocity) as a function of Reynolds number. When the weight is uneven across the pipe, the K-factor moves as the profile flattens with Re. For one two-coil geometry it falls from **0.1200** (laminar) to **0.0984** (flat profile), about 18%. A coil angle of **30°** makes it nearly independent of Re (**1.329–1.339**) (PDF p. 12). This is the pipe-flow form of a speed-dependent gain. Scope: "effects due to very high or very low liquid conductivity are ignored". |
| Shercliff (1956), "Edge effects in electromagnetic flowmeters," *J. Nucl. Energy* **3**(4), 305–311. [doi:10.1016/0891-3919(56)90058-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/0891-3919(56)90058-4) | `Shercliff_1956_Edge_Effects_Electromagnetic_Flowmeters.pdf` | Where the field ends, the induced EMF falls off and short-circuit currents close through the moving fluid, which lowers the sensitivity. In a rectangular duct the loss is "unimportant" once the pole-face aspect ratio c/b exceeds about 3 (PDF p. 3). The same currents distort the field in proportion to the magnetic Reynolds number (0.37R, about 4% for liquid sodium at R = 0.1). It is a liquid-metal paper. In seawater the induced currents are far weaker (our inference; the paper does not discuss electrolytes), so only the geometric edge loss, which carries no conductivity dependence, applies here. |
| Hemp & Versteeg (1988), "The effect of conducting pipes on electromagnetic flowmeter sensitivity," *J. Phys. D* **21**(7), 1091–1098. [doi:10.1088/0022-3727/21/7/007](https://doi.org/10.1088/0022-3727/21/7/007) | `Hemp_Versteeg_1988_Effect_Conducting_Pipes_Electromagnetic_Flowmeter_Sensitivity.pdf` | Puts numbers on a conductor near the sensing volume. Perfectly conducting pipe beginning one radius from the electrode plane lowers the K-factor by about **9%**. At 1.25, 1.5, 1.75, 2.0 and 2.5 radii the drop is about **4%, 2%, 0.7%, 0.3% and 0.07%** (PDF p. 8). The two limiting cases are steel in contact with the liquid and the same steel behind "an insulating deposit". Going from one to the other "represents the largest effect that the electrical properties of tubes can have on sensitivity" (PDF pp. 6–7). By implication, a gain can drift when nearby metal changes its electrical contact with the water through paint damage, corrosion or fouling, with nothing in the sensor changing. |
| Zhang & Hemp (1994), "Calculation of the virtual current around an electromagnetic velocity probe using the alternating method of Schwarz," *Flow Meas. Instrum.* **5**(3), 146–149. [doi:10.1016/0955-5986(94)90012-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/0955-5986(94)90012-4) | `Zhang_Hemp_1994_Calculation_Virtual_Current_Around_Electromagnetic_Velocity.pdf` | A semi-analytical (Schwarz alternating) solution for the virtual-current potential around a cylindrical probe facing a plane wall, with two point electrodes on its end face. It covers insulating and conducting walls and probe bodies, and converges in a few cycles to 0.01% in the series coefficients. The paper gives contour plots only, no sensitivities. Sensitivities follow in Zhang (2020, ch. 4), which reprints its conducting-probe figure. Companion to Hemp (1995) in table A. |
| Zhang (2020), "Electromagnetic velocity probe," ch. 4 in *Flow Measurement by Electromagnetic Induction: Theory and Numerical Methods*, IOP Publishing, 4-1–4-16. [doi:10.1088/978-0-7503-2564-6ch4](https://doi.org/10.1088/978-0-7503-2564-6ch4) | `Zhang_2020_Electromagnetic_Velocity_Probe.pdf` | 3D theory of a probe with point electrodes and a coil in its end face, near a wall. A conducting wall draws off virtual current and lowers the sensitivity, while a steel wall raises it through the field. A conducting probe body "will absorb considerable virtual current", and "a conducting probe is not good for the measurement" (PDF p. 10). With the wall closer than about **four** electrode spacings, the error can exceed **30%** for a ferromagnetic wall (PDF p. 25). The flow is potential flow throughout, with no boundary layer or wake, and the authors doubt the predicted rise near an insulating wall. It quotes Bevir that probe sensitivity "decays with the reciprocal fourth power of distance" (PDF p. 1), which is why only the first few electrode spacings around a head matter. |

## Field performance in waves and turbulence, and intercomparisons

What EM meters do once they leave the tow tank, and what a comparison with
another instrument can and cannot show. Careful users agree to a few cm s⁻¹
and about 10%. A zero error shows up only as a difference between flow
directions, or as a fractional bias that shrinks with speed. And at-sea
intercomparisons give "only relative performance". The last row covers the
acoustic reference: what can go wrong in a Nortek Signature1000 before it is
compared with `U_EM`.

| Bowden & White (1966), "Measurements of the orbital velocities of sea waves and their use in determining the directional spectrum," *Geophys. J. R. Astron. Soc.* **12**, 33–54. [doi:10.1111/j.1365-246X.1966.tb03100.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-246X.1966.tb03100.x) | `Bowden_White_1966_Measurements_Orbital_Velocities_Sea_Waves_Determining.pdf` | An early field check of an EM flowmeter against pressure and linear wave theory (Guza & Thornton 1980, below, cite it as such). The meter, calibrated in a water channel only over **0–60 cm/s** (PDF p. 3), measured all three orbital-velocity components 2.7 m above the bed in ~5.5 m of water off Gladstone Dock, Liverpool, with a pressure gauge on the bed; the spectral ratios were compared with first-order potential theory. Calibration error is put at up to **15%**, "independent of frequency" (PDF p. 12), yet the pressure-to-horizontal-velocity ratio still departs from theory at the lower wave frequencies (σ < 1.45 rad/s), significantly even allowing ±15% (PDF p. 13), and within about 1 m of the bed both pressure and horizontal velocity exceed what the 2.7 m record predicts (PDF p. 15). A frequency-dependent residual of that kind is what a single static gain cannot absorb (our reading). |
| McCullough (1979), *Near-Surface Ocean Current Sensors: Problems and Performance*, WHOI-79-90, 27 pp. [doi:10.1575/1912/10340](https://doi.org/10.1575/1912/10340) | `McCullough_1979_NearSurface_Current_Sensors.pdf` | A kinematic model that converts a sensor's measured steady vertical-cosine response into its error in mean speed under wave orbital flow. The controlling parameter is the ratio of mean to orbital speed, V₀/a_w. Below **1** the errors are large and depend on how the orbit is oriented relative to the mean flow (PDF pp. 11–15). In the measured dynamic responses, the electromagnetic and rotor-vane meters overestimate at low ratios (PDF p. 15). It attributes the CMICE differences to "the fixed-orientation mooring system and zero-stability properties of the electromagnetic current meters". It also warns that at-sea intercomparisons give no accuracy "since the required in situ flow standards do not exist. Only relative performance is directly observed" (PDF p. 24). |

Any one of p, u or η predicts the others' spectra to about **20%** in total variance; pairs away from the breakpoint usually agree within **10%**, with elevation overpredicting velocity. On the meter itself (PDF pp. 2–3): the sphere has a dc gain change near **80 cm/s** at boundary-layer transition; pre- and post-calibrations barely changed, so "the problem is not variation of gain during the period of the installation but an uncertainty about the dynamic response"; calibration gains varied slightly with the amplitude of an imposed sinusoid; and one gain from a 100 cm/s tow carries roughly **±5%** in amplitude (10% in variance). |
| Beardsley et al. (1981), "CMICE: A near-surface current meter intercomparison experiment," *Deep-Sea Res. A* **28**(12), 1577–1603. [doi:10.1016/0198-0149(81)90099-6](https://doi.org/10.1016/0198-0149(81)90099-6) | `Beardsley_1981_CMICE_Current_Meter_Intercomparison.pdf` | Twenty meters of seven types on six moorings in 27.8 m of water off Long Island. They include Marsh-McBirney cylindrical (711) and spherical (555) EM sensors on spars that keep the axes fixed. Properly working meters agreed "typically within ±5 cm s⁻¹ in speed and ±5°" for waves up to 3.5 m. The EM "speed calibration errors and zero shifts … may have been as large as ±3 cm s⁻¹" (PDF p. 12), and the spheres read **10–15%** below the cylinders above 20 cm s⁻¹ (PDF p. 15). The telling case is ECM 12: **30%** low against the VACMs in eastward flow, but within **3%** in westward flow (PDF p. 21). On fixed axes "errors in zero-point and non-linearity can accumulate" (PDF p. 14), and only a record with both flow directions exposes them. |
| Beardsley et al. (1986), "A VMCM S4 current meter intercomparison on a surface mooring in shallow water," *Proc. IEEE Third Working Conf. on Current Measurement*, 7–12. [doi:10.1109/CCM.1986.1158471](https://doi.org/10.1109/CCM.1986.1158471) | `Beardsley_1986_VMCM_S4_Current_Meter_Intercomparison_Surface.pdf` | An InterOcean S4 against two VMCMs on a surface mooring in Buzzards Bay. The S4 is a 25 cm sphere with flush electrodes, striated to force boundary-layer transition at very low speed. It read about **2 cm s⁻¹** high at 10–20 cm s⁻¹, with "the fractional speed difference decreas[ing] with increasing flow speed" (PDF p. 3), which is the pattern an additive offset produces (our reading). The authors instead model the VMCM's known **6%** under-response in waves; after that correction the S4 is **+2.6%** high for 0.5 < U/W < 2 (PDF p. 5). There is also a clock lesson: the S4's sampling clock gained **36 s/day**, and the record had to be re-timed from coherence and phase before it could be compared (PDF p. 2). |
| Clay & Longworth (1986), *A Performance Update of the InterOcean S4 Electromagnetic Current Meter*, WHOI-86-42. [doi:10.1575/1912/8015](https://doi.org/10.1575/1912/8015) | `Clay_Longworth_1986_S4_Performance_Update.pdf` | WHOI acceptance tests of three production S4s. Electrode half-cell potentials vary by "millivolts" against microvolt signals (PDF p. 9). Tow-tank speeds were "reasonably accurate, with some discrepancies introduced by the tank metallic frame" (PDF p. 16). The failure mode to remember: a fault in the depth circuit raised the current drain from **28 to 75 mA**. "Deterioration of the internal reference voltage caused by the depth circuit malfunction" then left only **20 of 32** days of reliable data (PDF pp. 22–24), so a fault outside the velocity channel corrupted it through shared electronics. Current drain "is one of the most obvious indications of a problem and should be checked periodically" (PDF p. 28). Its Buzzards Bay results summarize Beardsley et al. (1986). |
| MacVicar et al. (2007), "Measuring water velocity in highly turbulent flows: field tests of an electromagnetic current meter (ECM) and an acoustic Doppler velocimeter (ADV)," *Earth Surf. Process. [doi:10.1002/esp.1497](https://doi.org/10.1002/esp.1497) | `MacVicar_2007_Measuring_Water_Velocity_Highly_Turbulent_Flows.pdf` | A 13 mm Marsh-McBirney 523 beside a SonTek ADV in two gravel-bed rivers, at up to 1.75 m s⁻¹ and 50% turbulence intensity.
